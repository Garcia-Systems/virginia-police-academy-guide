# Glossary of Police and Academy Terminology

This selective glossary explains recurring professional terms; it does not replace current law, policy, or instruction. A cross-reference identifies the principal discussion, not every appearance.

## A

**AED (automated external defibrillator).** A device that analyzes heart rhythm and prompts a shock when appropriate. Use requires applicable training. See Chapter 15.

**Arrest.** A substantial seizure of a person under legal authority, ordinarily requiring probable cause. The permissible basis and procedure are fact-dependent. See Chapters 2 and 13.

**Authorized emergency vehicle.** A vehicle within Virginia's statutory category; any privilege depends on the vehicle, equipment, circumstances, law, and policy. See Chapter 12.

## C

**Call for service.** A request or report that prompts police attention. Its initial classification is not proof of what occurred. See Chapter 4.

**Certification.** Official status within Virginia's DCJS legal and administrative framework after applicable employment, training, examination, and record requirements are met. It is not simply an academy diploma. See Chapters 0, 18, and 19.

**Chain of custody.** The documented history of evidence collection, handling, transfer, storage, and safeguarding. See Chapter 6.

**Citation.** Commonly, written notice of an alleged violation. Use the document's exact Virginia name and distinguish it from a *summons*. See Chapter 5.

**Compulsory minimum training standards.** Minimum training requirements established through Virginia's statutory and regulatory system and administered by DCJS. The controlling version and effective date matter. See Chapters 0 and 18.

**Competency.** Demonstrated ability to satisfy a defined performance outcome. It does not mean legal jurisdiction or complete mastery. See Chapters 16–18.

**Consensual encounter.** An interaction a reasonable person would feel free to decline or end. Facts can change an encounter's legal character. See Chapter 2.

**CPR (cardiopulmonary resuscitation).** A trained emergency skill intended to support circulation during cardiac arrest. This book does not substitute for certification or practice. See Chapter 15.

**Critical incident.** A serious event with potential legal, investigative, operational, or wellness consequences. Agency definitions and procedures vary. See Chapter 11.

**Custody.** Lawful restriction of liberty that creates continuing duties concerning rights, safety, care, property, documentation, and transfer. It is not always synonymous with detention or arrest. See Chapters 8 and 13.

## D

**Deadly force.** Force carrying distinct legal consequences and limits under controlling law and policy; it is not synonymous with the mere presence of a firearm. See Chapters 9–11.

**De-escalation.** Feasible communication, time, distance, resources, and tactics used to reduce threat or intensity within law and policy. It is an ongoing process, not a guaranteed outcome. See Chapters 3 and 9.

**Dispatch.** The function that receives or coordinates calls, information, and resources under local procedure. See Chapters 3–4.

**Due regard.** The continuing legal responsibility to consider others' safety when exercising conditional emergency-driving privileges; it is not immunity. See Chapter 12.

**Duty to intervene.** A legal or policy obligation concerning another officer's unlawful force. Exact elements depend on current controlling authority. See Chapter 9.

## E

**EAP (Employee Assistance Program).** A workplace resource that may provide consultation or access to services. Availability, access, and confidentiality must be verified locally. See Chapters 20–21.

**EMS (Emergency Medical Services).** The coordinated system of prehospital emergency care. See Chapter 15.

**Evidence.** Information or material capable of supporting or refuting a fact. Relevance, admissibility, weight, and handling are distinct questions. See Chapter 6.

**Exigent circumstances.** A fact-dependent exception that may permit limited action without first obtaining a warrant when the law recognizes an urgent need. Current law controls its existence and scope. See Chapters 2 and 13.

## F

**Field training.** Structured, agency-supervised application and evaluation of academy learning in actual police work. Local models and duration vary. See Chapters 19–20.

**Field Training Officer (FTO).** An officer assigned to guide and evaluate a trainee's practical work. The title and local structure may vary. See Chapter 20.

## I

**In-service training.** Professional training after initial preparation, including applicable DCJS mandatory training and agency-specific learning. See Chapter 21.

**Investigative detention.** A limited seizure supported by reasonable suspicion and confined to its lawful justification. Duration and scope are fact-dependent. See Chapter 2.

## M

**Magistrate.** An independent Virginia judicial officer with defined powers involving matters such as probable-cause review, process, and bail; a magistrate is not in the police chain of command. See Chapter 13.

## N

**Naloxone.** Medication that can temporarily reverse opioid effects. It does not replace EMS activation, monitoring, or further care. See Chapter 15.

## O

**Objective reasonableness.** A constitutional standard assessing force from the perspective of a reasonable officer in the circumstances, rather than solely by subjective belief or hindsight. Application is intensely fact-dependent. See Chapters 2 and 9.

## P

**Patrol.** General police service in an assigned area or function; the word does not establish a particular deployment model. See Chapter 4.

**Peer support.** Support offered by colleagues prepared for that role. It does not automatically replace clinical care or carry identical confidentiality protections. See Chapters 20–21.

**Police academy.** A certified training institution or program delivering approved foundational law-enforcement education; published curriculum should not be confused with this book's editorial week sequence. See Chapter 0.

**Preliminary investigation.** Initial identification and collection of facts, preservation, interviews, leads, and documentation that frame later decisions. See Chapters 4 and 6.

**Probable cause.** Facts and circumstances sufficient to support the reasonable belief required for a particular legal decision, such as arrest or issuance of a search warrant. It is context-specific and less than proof beyond a reasonable doubt. See Chapter 2.

**Probationary officer.** An employment status defined by applicable law and agency rules; it does not automatically mean an uncertified recruit. See Chapters 19–21.

**Protective order.** A court order imposing specified protections. Its type, terms, service, duration, and current status must be verified. See Chapters 7 and 14.

## Q

**Qualification.** Documented performance of a defined minimum under prescribed conditions. It does not establish complete expertise or independent legal authority. See Chapters 10 and 18.

## R

**Reasonable suspicion.** Objective, particularized suspicion supported by specific facts and rational inferences, exceeding a hunch. See Chapter 2.

**Recruit.** A person in foundational academy preparation; employment, sworn, certification, and probation status must be described separately. See Chapters 0–1.

**Remediation.** Authorized additional instruction or practice addressing a demonstrated deficiency. It does not promise reassessment, a fixed number of attempts, or waiver of a requirement. See Chapters 16–18.

**Resistance.** A law-, training-, and policy-dependent description that should be connected to observable behavior rather than used as a self-proving label. See Chapters 8–9.

**Restraint.** An authorized means or condition limiting movement that creates continuing duties of safety, dignity, monitoring, and care; it is never punishment. See Chapter 8.

## S

**Scenario-based training.** Instruction or evaluation organized around a realistic problem. The controlling program determines whether an activity teaches, practices, or assesses. See Chapter 16.

**Search.** Government intrusion implicating a constitutionally protected privacy interest or area. Whether conduct is a search and what authority it requires depend on current law and facts. See Chapters 2 and 13.

**Search incident to lawful arrest.** A limited warrant exception linked to a lawful arrest; it is not general authority to search anything later associated with the person. See Chapter 13.

**Seizure.** Governmental interference with a person or property that satisfies the constitutional standard. Its occurrence and reasonableness are fact-dependent. See Chapter 2.

**Shift work.** Work organized into periods that may include days, evenings, or nights. The term does not establish JCCPD's schedule. See Chapters 20–21.

**Summons.** Legal process directing a person to appear or respond in court. It is not a conviction and is not always interchangeable with *citation*. See Chapters 2, 5, and 13.

## U

**Use of force.** An officer's physical coercion, governed by constitutional law, Virginia law, policy, training, documentation, and review. Labels alone do not establish lawfulness. See Chapter 9.

## V

**Vehicle pursuit.** A policy-defined effort to stop a driver who flees or refuses to stop; initiation, continuation, supervision, and termination require ongoing risk and necessity assessment. See Chapter 12.

**Victim.** A person reported or alleged to have suffered harm. Person-centered language does not itself decide credibility or a legal conclusion. See Chapter 7.

## W

**Warrant.** Judicial authorization ordinarily requiring probable cause, oath or affirmation, and particularity. Its terms and current law control. See Chapters 2 and 13.

**Witness.** A person who supplies information; the source and limits of the person's knowledge, including whether it is firsthand, should be identified. See Chapters 4 and 6.

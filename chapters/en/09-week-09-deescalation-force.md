# Chapter 9 — Week 9: De-escalation & Use-of-Force Decisions

> **Editorial sequence and research status.** Week 9 is this book's sequence, not a published HRCJTA schedule or JCCPD force policy. Authorities were reviewed for the August 20, 2026 edition. Force law changes and is intensely fact-dependent; current law, DCJS instruction, agency policy, and legal advice control. No physical technique is taught here.

## Opening

“Use of force” broadly describes an officer's exercise of physical coercion against a person. The phrase covers very different conduct and consequences, so an equipment name or category cannot answer whether a particular act was lawful. Authority, purpose, necessity, degree, duration, circumstances, and change all matter.

Professional judgment begins before force, continues throughout it, and does not stop afterward. Communication may gain cooperation. Distance, time, cover, or additional resources may create options when reasonably available. Immediate danger may make delay unsafe. Either way, the obligation is to perceive carefully, act lawfully, reassess, stop when justification ends, obtain care, report truthfully, and accept review.

## What This Week Is About

This chapter studies constitutional reasonableness, Virginia's statewide force provisions, de-escalation, decisions under uncertainty, intervention, medical response, reporting, and accountability. **Proportionality** is used as a judgment concept: the degree and duration of force should correspond to the lawful objective and circumstances, not operate as retaliation. Controlling constitutional and Virginia standards—not a standalone slogan—decide legality.

Virginia's force statutes are located in Title 19.2, Chapter 7, including §§ 19.2-83.3 through 19.2-83.5. Recruits must read the current text. Those provisions address prohibited neck restraints with a narrow statutory exception, intervention when another officer uses unlawful force, and statewide use-of-force requirements and data reporting. This book does not substitute its paraphrase for the statutes or an agency's more restrictive policy.

## What You Need to Understand

### *Graham v. Connor*: a standard, not a slogan

In *Graham v. Connor*, 490 U.S. 386 (1989), Dethorne Graham, who had diabetes, hurried into and out of a convenience store while seeking orange juice. An officer saw the behavior, made an investigative stop, and Graham was injured during the encounter. The lower court used a substantive-due-process test to evaluate his excessive-force claim.

The Supreme Court held that a claim of excessive force during an arrest, investigative stop, or other seizure is analyzed under the Fourth Amendment's **objective reasonableness** standard. The question is not the officer's private good or bad intention. Nor is it whether a different choice seems preferable after every fact is known. It is whether the force was objectively reasonable under the facts and circumstances confronting a reasonable officer on scene.

The Court identified considerations including the severity of the suspected crime, whether the person posed an immediate threat to officers or others, and whether the person was actively resisting arrest or attempting flight. The list is not a mechanical scorecard. The totality matters, and a fact can gain or lose importance as an encounter changes. The Court cautioned against judging through the “20/20 vision of hindsight,” while still requiring objective judicial review. That caution is not immunity from scrutiny and does not make an officer's assertion conclusive.

### *Tennessee v. Garner*: deadly force has special limits

In *Tennessee v. Garner*, 471 U.S. 1 (1985), an officer fatally shot an apparently unarmed teenager fleeing over a fence after a suspected nighttime burglary. The Court held that deadly force is a Fourth Amendment seizure and rejected a rule allowing it against every fleeing felony suspect. The Court explained that deadly force may not be used to prevent escape unless its constitutional conditions are met, centered on probable cause to believe the person poses a significant threat of death or serious physical injury to the officer or others; a warning should be given where feasible.

Current Virginia law adds statewide statutory requirements. A recruit must learn the exact current text and policy, including how the law addresses necessity, fleeing persons, warnings where feasible, de-escalation, cessation, medical assistance, prohibited practices, intervention, and reporting. Neither case supplies a tactical formula, and neither authorizes force merely because someone runs.

### De-escalation is disciplined judgment

De-escalation is a set of communication, assessment, coordination, and time-management tools used to seek a safer lawful resolution when circumstances reasonably permit. It can include calm tone, listening, plain expectations, fewer avoidable provocations, pauses, appropriate distance, reducing unnecessary stimulation, requesting an interpreter or crisis-trained/medical resource, and giving a person time to process.

It is not a magic script, a promise that cooperation will follow, or a requirement to ignore immediate danger. Options depend on the person, legal objective, environment, available resources, and threat. The professional question is not “Did I say the special phrase?” but “What reasonable options existed, what did I observe, and how did I adjust?”

### Decisions must remain open to change

```text
What do I know?
      ↓
What don't I know?
      ↓
What threat or safety concern exists?
      ↓
What legal authority do I have?
      ↓
What options are reasonably available?
      ↓
Has the situation changed?
      ↓
What must I document?
```

Uncertainty does not disappear because a decision is urgent. Separate observed fact from report, inference, and unknown. Reassess continually:

```text
Observe
   ↓
Assess
   ↓
Act lawfully
   ↓
Reassess
   ↓
Reduce or stop force when justification changes
```

Force is not one irreversible permission. A threat can increase, decrease, or end. Cooperation can begin. A medical emergency can become apparent. Continued force needs continued justification; force must be reduced or stopped when the justification changes or ends.

### Intervention and review protect people and the profession

Virginia § 19.2-83.4 creates a statutory intervention and reporting framework when a law-enforcement officer witnesses another officer's unlawful use of force, subject to the statute's feasibility and other terms. Read the operative text rather than relying on memory. The statute is distinct from DCJS training outcomes and from agency policy, which may add duties, routing, deadlines, or protections.

Intervention can require moral courage. Rank, friendship, embarrassment, or uncertainty about reception cannot convert unreasonable force into reasonable force. If safe and feasible under controlling requirements, act to stop the unlawful conduct and make the required report. Supervisory review is not betrayal; it is part of lawful public service.

### Care and reporting are part of the event

After force, address continuing danger, summon or provide appropriate medical assistance within training and policy, monitor the person, identify injuries or complaints, preserve evidence, notify required personnel, and cooperate with review. Never delay necessary care to finish an interview or improve a narrative.

A truthful report documents:

- the legal reason for the encounter;
- observable conduct and relevant statements;
- information known at each important decision point;
- communication, warnings, or attempted de-escalation where relevant;
- the type, degree, and duration of force in factual language;
- changing circumstances and reassessment;
- injuries, complaints, monitoring, and medical assistance;
- witnesses, recordings, physical evidence, and notifications; and
- what the officer did not know or could not observe.

Do not begin with a conclusion and retrofit facts to defend it. **Retrospective rationalization is not report writing.** Record what actually informed the decision at the time, including facts that complicate the account. A polished false justification is worse than an honest record of a difficult decision.

## What Training May Feel Like

Force analysis can be uncomfortable because reasonable people may notice different facts or reach different initial assessments. Scenario feedback should train articulation and reassessment, not reward certainty for its own sake. A recruit must tolerate correction, separate ego from judgment, and learn that restraint can be a successful outcome.

Physical force exercises belong under qualified supervision. This chapter intentionally omits methods, target areas, counters, restraint mechanics, and weapons tactics.

## Key Concepts and Vocabulary

- **Use of force:** physical coercion by an officer; law and policy distinguish kinds, purposes, and consequences.
- **Objective reasonableness:** Fourth Amendment assessment under the totality facing a reasonable officer on scene, not subjective intent or hindsight alone.
- **Deadly force:** force carrying the legal significance defined by controlling law and policy; it faces special constitutional and statutory limits.
- **Necessity:** whether force is needed to accomplish a lawful purpose under the circumstances.
- **De-escalation:** reasonable communication and tactical-judgment measures intended to reduce intensity and seek safer resolution; not passivity in immediate danger.
- **Proportionality:** conceptual fit between the degree/duration of force, lawful objective, and circumstances; apply controlling law.
- **Duty to intervene:** statutory and policy responsibility concerning another officer's unlawful force; exact elements control.
- **Reassessment:** renewed evaluation as information, behavior, threat, authority, or medical condition changes.
- **Retrospective rationalization:** inventing or reshaping reasons after the event; incompatible with truthful reporting.

## From the Classroom to the Street

**Fictional example—not JCCPD procedure.** A person reported for a low-level offense is shouting and pacing but has not threatened anyone. With no immediate danger, officers use one communicator, explain the lawful expectation, reduce competing commands, and request a suitable resource. The person begins cooperating. That outcome does not prove the same approach always works; it shows why offense severity, immediate threat, communication, time, and change matter.

In a second conceptual example, lawful force begins in response to assaultive conduct. The person then stops, follows directions, and develops an apparent medical problem. The justification and priorities change. Officers stop force that is no longer needed, request care, monitor, notify, preserve evidence, and report the full transition.

## From the Courthouse to the Academy

```text
Seconds to decide
       ↓
Hours to document
       ↓
Days or months of investigation
       ↓
Potential years of legal review
```

Reports, body-worn-camera footage where applicable, dispatch records, witness statements, physical and medical evidence, policy, statutes, and constitutional law may later be compared by supervisors, investigators, prosecutors, defense counsel, judges, juries, and civil courts. Video can be powerful yet may not reproduce every perspective or perception; neither memory nor video should be overstated.

Slow review of a fast event is not inherently unfair. *Graham* requires attention to the on-scene circumstances, while accountability requires testing the officer's account against the whole reliable record. The lesson is disciplined judgment and candor, not fear of review.

## What May Be Difficult

**Urgency without certainty:** name what is unknown. **Authority without ego:** compliance is not personal respect. **Changing course:** stopping or reducing force is good judgment, not indecision. **Peer intervention:** prepare to act professionally across rank and friendship. **Writing:** chronology must show what was known when. **Public concern:** explain lawful standards without dismissing the human consequence.

## Questions to Think About

1. What did *Graham* decide, and what did it not decide?
2. Which facts bear on severity, immediate threat, resistance, or flight?
3. What options were reasonably available before and during force?
4. When did the circumstances change?
5. What requires intervention, care, notification, or preservation?
6. Does the report distinguish contemporary reasons from hindsight?
7. What evidence might confirm or challenge the account?

## Week in Review

Force is an exercise of government authority subject to constitutional, statutory, policy, ethical, and public review. Assess the whole situation, use de-escalation when reasonably possible, do not ignore immediate danger, match decisions to lawful needs, reassess, stop when justification ends, intervene as required, obtain medical care, and document facts without retrofitting a defense.

# Chapter 14 — Week 14: Domestic Calls & High-Risk Encounters

> **Editorial sequence and safety boundary.** Week 14 is this book's sequence, not a published HRCJTA schedule. Sources were reviewed for the August 20, 2026 edition. “High-risk” describes elevated danger or uncertainty; it does not introduce entry, ambush, positioning, disarming, or assault-response tactics. Current law, supervised training, and policy control.

## Opening

At a domestic call, two people may describe the same minutes differently. A child may be nearby. An injury may be visible, hidden, old, or absent. One person may be frightened of the other, of arrest, of losing housing, or of what happens after police leave. A weapon may be reported but not observed. Careful work neither dismisses danger nor converts uncertainty into fact.

## What This Week Is About

This chapter concerns family abuse, Virginia arrest duties, protective orders, victim behavior, children, evidence, de-escalation, and elevated-risk calls. Its center is lawful, trauma-informed assessment: gather information, address immediate safety, distinguish reports from observations, request appropriate resources, document, and connect people to services.

## What You Need to Understand

### Domestic calls combine law, history, and continuing relationships

Participants may give conflicting accounts while emotions are intense. Financial dependence, disability, immigration concerns, isolation, housing, shared parenting, prior harm, threats, and fear of retaliation can shape what a person says or is ready to do. Relationships often continue after officers leave. Police must not demand a stereotyped “ideal victim” response.

Virginia's term **family or household member** has the specific definition in § 16.1-228. It includes listed relatives and certain people who cohabit or cohabited within the preceding 12 months, whether or not they have lived together continuously, as the full statute explains. Everyday use of “domestic” cannot replace that definition.

Section 18.2-57.2 defines and penalizes assault and battery against a family or household member and includes offense-specific provisions. It does not make every argument or troubling relationship a criminal offense. Probable cause must attach to legally defined conduct and the proper person.

### Virginia imposes particular arrest responsibilities

Virginia Code § 19.2-81.3 governs arrests for certain family-abuse crimes and protective-order violations. When an officer has probable cause to believe specified offenses occurred, the statute directs arrest subject to its terms. Where probable cause exists to believe two or more family or household members committed assault and battery against one another, the officer generally arrests only the **predominant physical aggressor**, unless the statutory circumstances support arrest of additional persons.

The statute instructs consideration of specified factors, including prior complaints, relative severity of injuries, whether injuries were inflicted in self-defense, witness statements, and other observations. “Predominant physical aggressor” is a statutory assessment, not “who is larger,” “who called first,” “who is calmer,” or an invitation to arrest both people automatically. The current text, probable cause, and training control every case.

Section 19.2-81.3 also addresses petitioning for an emergency protective order and providing information about community resources in covered circumstances. Agency forms, notifications, victim-service coordination, and arrest processing remain policy matters.

### Protective orders have distinct stages

Virginia's family-abuse framework includes:

- an **emergency protective order** under § 16.1-253.4, issued by an authorized judge or magistrate upon the statutory showing; its duration is short and calculated under the statute, including provisions for days when court is not in session;
- a **preliminary protective order** under § 16.1-253.1, issued pending a hearing when statutory requirements are met; and
- a protective order after hearing under § 16.1-279.1, which may remain effective for a period stated by the court within statutory limits and can contain authorized conditions.

These orders differ in issuance, notice, hearing, duration, and relief. Other Virginia protective-order chapters may apply outside family abuse. Officers verify the order, service and enforceability information, exact conditions, dates, and current law. Violation consequences are addressed in § 16.1-253.2 and related law. This overview is not advice to a petitioner or respondent.

Federal and Virginia firearms disabilities can follow particular qualifying orders or convictions. Because status, notice, findings, relationship, duration, and exceptions matter, officers and affected people must use the actual order and current legal guidance rather than a general statement.

### Evidence matters even when participation changes

Evidence can include attributed statements; officers' observations; injury and scene photographs under policy; damaged property; witness accounts; calls, messages, or recordings lawfully obtained; body-worn-camera material where applicable; protective-order and prior-incident records; and medical evidence obtained through lawful process. A victim's later reluctance does not erase an officer's duty to document the evidence that existed. Nor does an allegation prove itself.

Trauma may affect sequence, detail, memory, emotion, and willingness to disclose. A victim may minimize, delay, remain in the relationship, decline a referral, or fear practical consequences. Those responses are not automatic proof of dishonesty—or automatic proof of the allegation. Use patient, nonjudgmental questions, accurate attribution, corroboration, and the evidence principles from Chapters 6 and 7.

### Children require safety and appropriate specialization

Children may be injured, threatened, used to control another person, or affected by exposure even when they are not the direct target. Address immediate medical and safety needs, document what is observed and spontaneously said, and avoid repetitive or unnecessary questioning. Specialized forensic interviews belong to trained personnel.

Virginia Code § 63.2-1509 lists law-enforcement officers among mandated reporters who must promptly report when, in their professional capacity, they have reason to suspect a child is abused or neglected. The statute controls timing, destination, information, and consequences; current agency and child-welfare procedures govern implementation. A report is a protective referral, not a final adjudication of abuse.

### Elevated risk does not eliminate legal analysis

Reports of weapons, violent disturbance, serious threats, wanted persons, or rapidly changing conduct raise concern. They should prompt information gathering, clear communication, appropriate resources and supervision, attention to the public, and continued reassessment. When circumstances permit, slowing events, listening, setting clear expectations, and avoiding unnecessary provocation can create safer choices. Immediate danger may sharply limit time and options.

A weapon report remains **reported information** until supported or contradicted. Record the source, detail, timing, reliability indicators, actual observations, verified facts, and uncertainty. Weapon presence may be important to danger and legal authority, but it does not automatically decide who committed an offense or whether every search, seizure, arrest, or force option is lawful.

### De-escalation is purposeful communication

A calm voice, respectful listening, simple expectations, time when reasonably available, conceptually separate conversations when safe and appropriate, and help from a supervisor, crisis professional, victim advocate, interpreter, or other specialist may reduce volatility. De-escalation is not a promise that everyone will calm down and not a requirement to ignore imminent harm. It is the disciplined use of reasonable opportunities consistent with Virginia law, training, and policy.

## What Training May Feel Like

Classroom and scenario work may feel emotionally dense. Recruits must hear competing accounts, notice legal elements, communicate without taking sides prematurely, consider children and medical needs, identify evidence, and write an intelligible chronology. A scenario can be difficult without being a tactical spectacle.

## Key Concepts and Vocabulary

- **Family abuse:** Virginia statutory term defined in § 16.1-228; do not substitute a colloquial definition.
- **Family or household member:** defined Virginia category controlling whether family-abuse provisions apply.
- **Predominant physical aggressor:** statutory assessment under § 19.2-81.3 when reciprocal probable cause exists; not a demographic stereotype.
- **Emergency protective order:** short-duration judicial order under the applicable statute, distinct from preliminary and post-hearing orders.
- **Trauma-informed:** communication attentive to possible trauma without presuming facts or credibility.
- **Mandatory report:** legally required report of suspected child abuse or neglect; not a final finding.
- **High-risk encounter:** this chapter's broad description of elevated danger or uncertainty, not a universal agency classification.

## From the Classroom to the Street

**Illustrative and nontactical.** Dispatch reports an argument and a possible weapon. On arrival, accounts conflict and no weapon has been verified. An officer treats the report seriously while separating reported, observed, and unknown information; requests suitable support; checks immediate safety and medical concerns; determines relationships and legal elements; checks order information; considers children; gathers lawful evidence; and reassesses. No call label supplies probable cause or a tactical answer.

## From the Courthouse to the Academy

```text
First report and officer observations
              ↓
Probable-cause and safety decisions
              ↓
Arrest / complaint / emergency protective order
              ↓
Bond conditions and victim-witness contact
              ↓
Hearing, testimony, or later alleged violation
```

The protective order, warrant, complaint, bond condition, photographs, service record, and testimony later seen in court grow from the first response. Exact names, relationships, quoted words, observations, probable-cause facts, order details, referrals, and service or enforcement actions matter. Courthouse familiarity helps explain the destination; it does not predetermine the street decision.

## What May Be Difficult

**Conflicting stories:** uncertainty must be documented, not hidden. **Victim autonomy and legal duty:** a person's wishes matter, but statutory arrest or reporting duties may control. **Children:** concern must not become amateur forensic interviewing. **Repetition:** repeated calls can invite cynicism. **Emotional load:** studying threats, coercion, children, and recurring harm can leave a recruit mentally tired even when the work was classroom-based.

## Questions to Think About

1. What relationships and statutory definitions apply?
2. What is reported, observed, verified, inferred, or unknown?
3. Is anyone in immediate danger or in need of medical care?
4. What facts support probable cause, self-defense, or predominant-aggressor analysis?
5. Is a protective order valid, served where required, current, and applicable?
6. Are children or vulnerable people present, and is a report required?
7. What resources and documentation will remain useful after officers leave?

## Week in Review

Domestic calls require patience without passivity. Virginia law supplies exact definitions, arrest duties, predominant-aggressor considerations, protective-order processes, and child-reporting duties. Trauma can shape behavior without deciding truth. Weapon information raises concern but does not erase proof, legal authority, or uncertainty. Communication, resources, evidence, referrals, and accurate documentation connect immediate safety to later justice.

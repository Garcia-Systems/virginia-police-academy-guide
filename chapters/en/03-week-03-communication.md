# Chapter 3 — Week 3: Communication, Radio & Report Writing

## Opening

Police work is conducted in words as much as in motion. A caller describes something incomplete. Dispatch compresses it for radio. An officer asks questions, gives directions, listens for changes, takes notes, and later writes a record that may be read years afterward. Every transfer can preserve meaning—or distort it.

A report is not paperwork completed after the “real” work. It is part of the work: a durable account that lets other people understand what the officer perceived, decided, did, and preserved.

## What This Week Is About

This book places communication early because law becomes action through communication. An officer must obtain information without promising an outcome, explain authority without arguing endlessly, give a lawful instruction clearly, use radio time responsibly, and document facts without pretending to know more than the evidence shows.

Virginia’s DCJS performance outcomes include communication, interviewing, report writing, and legal articulation in the compulsory-minimum framework.[1] The exact local systems, signals, phrasing, required fields, and approval process must come from current HRCJTA and JCCPD instruction.

## What You Need to Understand

### Listening is an investigative skill

Listening is not silence while planning the next question. It involves attending to words, sequence, uncertainty, emotion, and what has not yet been established. Begin with an open invitation when circumstances permit: “Tell me what happened.” Then narrow:

- Who was present?
- What did you personally see or hear?
- What happened next?
- Where were you standing?
- How do you know that person’s identity?
- What are you unsure about?

Avoid planting facts in a witness’s account. “Was the car red?” can contaminate memory when “Describe the vehicle” was available. Clarify pronouns, relative times (“a little while”), distances, lighting, and whether a statement is personal observation or something heard from another person.

### Different people require different care, not different truth

**Victims** may be frightened, injured, ashamed, angry, or concerned they will not be believed. Explain immediate steps, preserve dignity, avoid promises about arrest or prosecution, and distinguish needed questions from blame.

**Witnesses** may be confident and wrong, reluctant, distracted, or influenced by one another. Separate accounts when lawful and practical; document uncertainty and the person’s own words.

**Suspects** retain constitutional protections. Professional questioning is not permission for coercion, threats, fabricated legal authority, or ignoring invocation of rights. Custody and interrogation require the legal analysis introduced in Chapter 2.

**Distressed people** may process language slowly or differently because of crisis, disability, language access needs, intoxication, injury, fear, or age. Reduce unnecessary stimulation when safe, use plain language, allow processing time, and seek appropriate medical, interpreter, crisis, or supervisory resources under policy.

A professional introduction can lower ambiguity: identify yourself and agency, state the immediate purpose when it is lawful and safe to do so, and explain what you need. Respect does not eliminate authority; it can make authority easier to understand.

### De-escalation is purposeful communication and time management

Virginia law defines and requires de-escalation in the use-of-force context when feasible, including efforts to reduce immediacy of a threat so that more time, options, and resources may become available.[2] De-escalation is not a promise that words will solve every event and not a demand to abandon lawful action. It can include calm tone, distance and time when feasible, active listening, clear options, one officer speaking, requests for support, and avoiding unnecessary provocation.

A lawful instruction should be specific, audible, realistic, and consistent where circumstances permit. State the action: “Please keep your hands where I can see them,” not merely “Stop acting suspicious.” Allow reasonable time to process unless urgency prevents it. If noncompliance matters, document the instruction, opportunity to comply, observed response, and surrounding conditions—not simply “refused commands.”

### Radio communication is shared, perishable space

The radio links officers and dispatch while everyone is managing other tasks. Good radio discipline generally means:

- listen before transmitting;
- know what essential information must go first;
- speak clearly, at a controlled pace;
- use the agency’s taught identifiers and terminology;
- keep routine traffic concise without omitting safety-critical facts;
- acknowledge and correct misunderstood information;
- avoid names or sensitive details over channels when policy uses another method;
- do not invent codes or imitate entertainment media.

A useful mental structure is **identity/location → event or request → essential description/direction → updates**. It is only a learning aid, not JCCPD protocol. Local radio procedure, emergency traffic, status checks, channels, computer-aided dispatch, phonetic alphabet, signals, and interoperability are agency-specific.

### Notes support memory; reports support accountability

Contemporaneous notes should preserve times, names and identifiers, exact significant words, observations, actions, evidence, locations, and sources of information according to policy. Mark quotation accurately. Do not use quotation marks around a paraphrase. Do not destroy or rewrite original notes contrary to retention and discovery requirements.

A strong report usually lets a reader answer:

1. Why did the officer become involved?
2. Who supplied each fact?
3. What did the officer personally observe?
4. What happened in chronological order?
5. What legal decision was made, and which facts supported it at that time?
6. What statements were made and under what relevant conditions?
7. What evidence was located, handled, photographed, transferred, or submitted?
8. What follow-up, notification, aid, or disposition occurred?

Agency format may reorganize these answers. Follow the required report system rather than forcing a private template into it.

## What Training May Feel Like

Communication practice can feel harder than conversation because an instructor may evaluate several channels at once. You are listening to a person, monitoring radio traffic, watching behavior, remembering a description, choosing lawful words, and preparing to write later. Physical fatigue narrows attention. Anger can pull you toward argument. Fear can speed speech. Several people talking can make the loudest voice seem most reliable even when it is not.

Repeated practice is meant to make a structure available under load: pause, identify the immediate priority, ask one clear question or give one clear instruction, confirm critical information, and record it. Afterward, compare notes with available recordings and evidence rather than assuming memory is complete.

## Key Concepts and Vocabulary

- **Active listening:** deliberate attention, clarification, and confirmation of meaning.
- **Open-ended question:** invites a narrative rather than suggesting a short or preferred answer.
- **Closed question:** seeks a specific detail; useful for clarification after an account is obtained.
- **Leading question:** suggests the desired answer and may affect reliability.
- **Dispatch:** the communications function coordinating calls, information, and resources under local procedure.
- **Radio discipline:** concise, clear, prioritized use of a shared communications channel.
- **Articulation:** factual explanation of the basis for a decision.
- **Objective language:** descriptions grounded in observable or attributed facts rather than insults, stereotypes, or unsupported conclusions.
- **Inference:** a reasoned conclusion drawn from facts; it should be identified and supported, not written as direct observation.
- **Chronology:** events organized by time and sequence.
- **Attribution:** identifying who said, saw, supplied, or did something.
- **De-escalation:** communication, time, distance, resources, and other tactics used when feasible to reduce threat or intensity within law and policy.

## From the Classroom to the Street

### Fictional educational example—not a JCCPD report format

**Weak:**

> The guy was acting suspicious so I stopped him. He was uncooperative and had burglary tools.

The paragraph hides every important decision. “Suspicious,” “uncooperative,” and “burglary tools” are conclusions. It does not say what the officer observed, when a detention began, what was said, or how the objects were found.

**Stronger articulation:**

> At 2:14 a.m., dispatch assigned me to a glass-break alarm at Green Street Pharmacy, which was closed. As I turned into the pharmacy’s rear lot, I saw one person standing approximately ten feet from a rear door. The person was pulling the door handle with both hands. I saw fresh broken glass on the pavement below the door and heard the alarm sounding. The person looked toward my marked vehicle and ran east behind the building. I radioed my location and the person’s clothing description. I then directed the person to stop. These observations, together with the dispatched alarm, led me to detain the person while I investigated the apparent break-in.

This version does not prove every later element and deliberately does not describe a search or arrest. It shows the principle: **document observable facts before conclusions**. Times, distances, quotations, and other details must be recorded honestly; never add precision that was not observed or measured.

### Observation versus inference

- **Observation:** “Her hands shook, tears were visible, and she spoke in short sentences.”
- **Unsupported label:** “She was hysterical.”
- **Attributed statement:** “Lee said, ‘I saw the blue sedan strike the mailbox.’”
- **Inference clearly stated:** “Based on the odor, the wet paint transfer, and Lee’s statement, I believed the blue sedan had contacted the mailbox.”

The inference may be reasonable, but the report lets the reader evaluate its foundation.

### Explain decisions without debating them away

An officer should provide an understandable explanation when lawful and feasible: the reason for contact, what will happen next, and what options exist. But explanation is not endless roadside litigation. Listen to relevant information, correct a misunderstanding, state the decision professionally, and follow current law and policy. Sarcasm and threats make information less reliable and review more difficult.

## From the Courthouse to the Academy

A report may be used by:

- a supervisor deciding whether the investigation is complete;
- a prosecutor evaluating charges and disclosure obligations;
- defense counsel testing the basis and reliability of police action;
- a judge deciding probable cause, admissibility, or credibility;
- a witness trying to recall an event months later;
- a clerk docketing and maintaining the case record;
- a later investigator finding a connection the original officer could not know;
- the officer preparing to testify.

Your clerk experience already shows that documents outlive the moment. In the academy, reverse the view: the report begins with notes and disciplined attention at the scene. Write for a reader who was not there. A report should neither advocate for conviction nor minimize incriminating evidence. It should accurately preserve material facts, including facts that complicate the officer’s first theory.

## What May Be Difficult

### Cognitive load

When several people speak, radio traffic arrives, information is incomplete, and the officer is tired, working memory fails predictably. Countermeasures are procedural: prioritize immediate safety and medical needs, control the pace when feasible, repeat critical information, separate sources, take lawful notes, use body-worn or dispatch records as policy permits, and seek help.

### Writing too much—and too little

A long report can still omit legal facts; a concise report can still be complete. Include material detail and source attribution. Remove filler, boilerplate that is not true of this event, jokes, diagnoses outside your competence, and character judgments. Explain abbreviations on first use unless the approved system defines them.

### Quoting and paraphrasing

Exact words may matter for threats, admissions, consent, requests for counsel, or excited statements. Use quotation marks only when reasonably confident of the wording and preserve context. Otherwise write “in substance” or clearly paraphrase, following instruction and policy.

### Proofreading your own certainty

Before submission, compare names, dates, times, addresses, pronouns, property identifiers, and statute numbers with source records. Search for conclusions such as “obviously,” “aggressive,” “furtive,” “refused,” or “appeared intoxicated” and ask which facts support them. Confirm that the sequence explains when authority changed.

## Questions to Think About

1. Which question invites a witness’s memory rather than supplying an answer?
2. How would you communicate one essential update in ten seconds without using invented codes?
3. What observable facts might support the word “angry”? Is the label necessary?
4. How would you document a command, the person’s opportunity to understand it, and the response?
5. Which facts in a report came from dispatch, a witness, another officer, or your own observation?
6. What detail could help defense counsel as well as the prosecutor, and why must it still be included?
7. What does your agency require when notes, recordings, and memory differ?

## Week in Review

Communication is a continuous evidence and accountability process. Listen before narrowing; distinguish each speaker’s knowledge; adapt respectfully to distress; give lawful instructions clearly; and treat radio space as a shared resource. Notes preserve information, while reports organize observations, attributed statements, decisions, evidence, and outcomes. Local radio and report rules were not publicly verified and must come from HRCJTA and JCCPD. Above all, document observable facts before conclusions.

# Chapter 8 — Week 8: Defensive Tactics & Custody

> **Editorial sequence and research status.** Week 8 is this book's learning sequence, not a claim about HRCJTA's calendar or JCCPD procedure. Authorities were reviewed for the August 20, 2026 edition; current law, academy instruction, agency policy, supervision, and medical direction control. This chapter explains responsibilities, not physical-control techniques.

## Opening

An arrest can look like an ending: authority established, person restrained, problem solved. Professionally, it is a beginning. Once the state restricts a person's freedom, the officer assumes significant responsibility for that person's safety, rights, property, and treatment. The work includes a lawful basis, communication, restraint judgment, health assessment, transport, property accounting, documentation, transfer, and review.

Custody is therefore not simply “getting control.” It is the exercise of public authority over a human being who may be frightened, angry, injured, intoxicated, confused, disabled, or ill. Dignity and vigilance belong together.

## What This Week Is About

In this book, Week 8 introduces the legal, professional, and human responsibilities involved in taking someone into custody. **Defensive tactics** names a supervised field of academy learning—not instructions in this book. Recruits must learn what authorized control skills are for, when any physical intervention is lawful, how policy limits it, and how to recognize when the need changes. Only qualified instructors can teach and evaluate physical performance safely.

**Detention** and **arrest** both restrict liberty, but they are not interchangeable. As Chapter 2 explains, an investigative detention requires its own constitutional justification and scope; an arrest generally requires probable cause and applicable authority. Labels do not decide the issue. What the officer does, how long and how intensely liberty is restricted, and the circumstances matter.

## What You Need to Understand

### Authority comes before control

Before taking custody, identify the legal authority: a warrant, probable cause and applicable arrest authority, a court order, or another lawful basis. Virginia arrest statutes contain fact-specific rules and exceptions. Academy and agency instruction must supply current application; this chapter does not turn § 19.2-81 into a field checklist.

When circumstances reasonably permit, clear communication may avoid physical intervention. Identify yourself and the lawful expectation; use understandable language; listen for confusion; allow a reasonable opportunity to comply; and account for hearing, speech, cognitive, mobility, language, and medical needs. Qualified language assistance or another resource may be necessary. Communication cannot always resolve immediate danger, but haste, insults, contradictory commands, or avoidable confrontation can create needless risk.

### Resistance terms require definitions

Training materials sometimes distinguish **noncompliance**, **passive resistance**, **active resistance**, and **assaultive behavior**. These are general descriptors unless a controlling statute, standard, or policy defines them. They must not be treated as verified JCCPD categories. Describe observable conduct—words, movement, actions, and changes—rather than assigning a conclusion alone. A person who does not respond may be refusing, but may instead be unable to hear, understand, breathe, or process an instruction.

Any response must have lawful purpose and remain reasonable in light of the circumstances. Physical control is not punishment, retaliation, or a way to compel respect. As circumstances change, the officer must reassess the need for restraint and force under current law and policy.

### Restraints create continuing obligations

Handcuffs and other authorized restraints can limit movement, reduce identified risk, and facilitate lawful custody. They are not punishment. Selection, application, fit, positioning, and removal are hands-on matters for supervised instruction and agency policy—not this book.

The responsibility does not end when a restraint is applied. Observe the person, respond to breathing difficulty, altered awareness, injury, pain, or other distress, request medical help when indicated, and communicate relevant information at transfer. A restrained person's complaint deserves assessment rather than sarcasm or an automatic assumption of deception. Restraint never converts a person into cargo.

### Custody can involve urgent medical risk

Breathing difficulty, chest pain, loss of consciousness, altered mental status, injury, intoxication, severe agitation, extreme fatigue, temperature exposure, and a marked change in behavior can require prompt medical evaluation. No single appearance establishes a cause. Officers are not expected to diagnose; they are expected to recognize concern, summon EMS or other appropriate care, follow trained direction, and keep monitoring.

The U.S. Department of Justice has warned in its law-enforcement guidance that restraint-related deaths require attention to breathing and medical distress. Contemporary emergency-medicine organizations prefer descriptive language such as **severe agitation with altered mental status** over treating controversial labels as a diagnosis. This book gives no restraint-position instructions: current academy training, policy, and medical direction govern.

Transport and holding add responsibilities. Confirm the receiving location and applicable acceptance requirements; protect the person and vehicle occupants; account for property; do not expose confidential information unnecessarily; document material observations and medical care; and obtain a clear transfer of custody. Exact search, vehicle, property, observation, and handoff procedures are agency-specific and are not asserted here.

### A search is a legal event, not a routine slogan

A lawful custodial arrest can support a search incident to arrest under Fourth Amendment doctrine, including the officer-safety and evidence-preservation rationales recognized in *United States v. Robinson*, 414 U.S. 218 (1973). Its permissible scope is governed by current case law and the facts. Different authority may govern an investigative frisk, inventory, warrant search, consent search, or search of digital information. *Riley v. California*, 573 U.S. 373 (2014), illustrates why the arrest label does not erase constitutional limits: police generally need a warrant to search digital information on a seized cell phone, absent an applicable exception.

This is a conceptual bridge to Chapter 2, not a search procedure. Recruits must learn the distinctions from current legal instruction and policy.

## What Training May Feel Like

Supervised defensive-tactics practice may be physically demanding. Repetition, close contact, correction, and lawful role-play can produce ordinary soreness and emotional fatigue. Follow instructor safety rules, report injury or illness promptly, maintain hygiene, hydrate and recover as directed, and do not rehearse academy control methods with family or friends. Concealing an injury helps neither the recruit nor a training partner.

The harder adjustment may be ethical: an officer can be firm and decisive while treating a resisting or accused person as a person. Professional control includes control of one's own anger, ego, and language.

## Key Concepts and Vocabulary

- **Custody:** government restriction of a person's freedom under lawful authority, carrying safety, rights, care, property, and documentation responsibilities.
- **Detention:** a limited seizure requiring legal justification; not automatically an arrest.
- **Arrest:** custody to answer for an offense under applicable legal authority.
- **Compliance / voluntary compliance:** conduct meeting a lawful expectation without physical compulsion; communication can support it when circumstances permit.
- **Resistance:** context-dependent training or policy terminology that should be tied to observable behavior and controlling definitions.
- **Restraint:** authorized equipment or control that limits movement; never punishment.
- **Defensive tactics:** supervised professional training in lawful physical-control and safety skills; this chapter provides no mechanics.
- **Duty of care:** the practical and legal responsibilities that accompany control over another person.
- **Transfer of custody:** documented handoff of the person, property, records, and material safety or medical information.

## From the Classroom to the Street

**Fictional example—not JCCPD policy.** An officer has lawful authority to arrest a person who initially does not move. The officer notices the person looking toward an interpreter application and giving confused answers. With no immediate danger requiring faster action, the officer simplifies the explanation and obtains qualified language help according to policy. The person complies voluntarily. The lesson is not that talking always works; it is that untested assumptions can turn confusion into unnecessary physical intervention.

In another conceptual example, a restrained person becomes less responsive and appears to have trouble breathing during transport preparation. The custody task changes. The officer summons medical assistance, continues observation, relays the known timeline and observations, and documents the response. A completed arrest does not outrank a medical emergency.

## From the Courthouse to the Academy

```text
Legal authority
      ↓
Officer decision
      ↓
Custody / restraint
      ↓
Medical and safety responsibilities
      ↓
Documentation
      ↓
Possible court review
```

Supervisors, prosecutors, defense attorneys, judges, internal investigators, and civil courts may later examine authority, video where available, witness accounts, restraint decisions, medical records, property records, and reports. The officer should be able to state factually what happened, what was perceived, what authority applied, how circumstances changed, what care was requested, and how custody ended or transferred.

Your courthouse experience may make the finished warrant or docket familiar. Academy learning adds the human interval between legal authorization and court appearance. Every link can affect admissibility, safety, credibility, and public trust.

## What May Be Difficult

**Separating authority from emotion:** lawful custody never authorizes humiliation. **Reading inability accurately:** silence or unusual movement may reflect language, disability, fear, or illness. **Continuing observation:** do not let completed paperwork replace attention to a person. **Property:** small omissions can become serious disputes. **Neutral description:** record behavior rather than importing a label. **Asking for help:** medical and supervisory consultation is sound judgment, not weakness.

## Questions to Think About

1. What lawful authority supports the restriction of liberty?
2. What could apparent noncompliance mean besides refusal?
3. What changed after restraint, and what did that change require?
4. Which observations suggest medical evaluation?
5. How will property and transfer be accounted for?
6. Is the search authority distinct from the arrest authority?
7. Would the report let a later reviewer reconstruct care as well as control?

## Week in Review

Custody is an assumption of responsibility. Begin with lawful authority and clear communication; seek voluntary compliance when reasonably possible; use only trained, authorized, reasonable measures; monitor health; safeguard rights and property; transport and transfer responsibly; and document honestly. Restraints are not punishment. A person's vulnerability increases—not reduces—the importance of professionalism.
